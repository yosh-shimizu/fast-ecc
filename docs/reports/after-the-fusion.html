After the fusion: the 5-tap gradient, the folded normalisation, and the laplacian column

Three changes that landed after the fused Gauss–Newton stage (PR #4, #5), and the study they came out of: what the first-order model of the ECC paper leaves free, and what the rest of an iteration was spending its time on.

In one paragraph. With the Gauss–Newton stage fused, an iteration of fastecc::findTransformECC spent 45–60 % of its time on bookkeeping (masking, means, norms, the recombination) rather than on the warp or the algebra. Folding that bookkeeping into two passes that write nothing made an iteration 1.34–1.56× cheaper, exactly. Separately, the forward-additive step of the paper is the exact maximiser of its first-order model, so the only estimated quantity left in that model is the image gradient, and its bias sets the convergence rate: the 4th-order 5-tap reaches the accuracy floor in 3 iterations instead of 4–5 at no cost. And an experiment on estimating the pre-filter's sigma alongside the motion — which cannot work, the objective has no maximum in sigma — left behind a column that does: the laplacian of the warped image, carried as a nuisance direction, halves the first step, widens the basin by 10–40 % and moves the fixed point on a real image 2.6× closer to the truth. Both are on by default now.

"Bookkeeping" is everything but the image warp and the Gauss–Newton pass: the nearest-neighbour warp of the mask, the two masked meanStdDev, two masked subtract, two countNonZero, three masked setTo, the two addWeighted of the A−T recombination, and a dot product. Each is a masked pass over one to three planes that also writes a plane. For translation the means and norms alone cost as much as the warp. Anything that approximates the Hessian could touch at most the 12–34 % that the GN column holds.

2. The gradient estimate sets the convergence rate

The paper's step (its eq. 19) is the closed-form global maximiser of the correlation coefficient of the linearised warped image, iw(p+Δp)≈ iw(p)+GΔp. Within that model there is nothing to iterate: the only quantity that is estimated rather than exact is the gradient that builds G. If an estimate G^=G (1+δ) is used, the error near the solution contracts by about δ per iteration — linear convergence on a noise-free scene is the signature of a biased Jacobian, and the 3-tap central difference is biased at the high frequencies where the gradient energy sits.

  • The fixed point does not move: started at the ground truth, corner error and bias t-values agree to four digits across the three kernels. The residual is below the floor, so the solution of G^Tr=0 is the same.

The change is one line, (1, −8, 0, 8, −1)/12 for (−1, 0, 1)/2, plus a mask ring wide enough that no gradient inside the mask is formed from reflected samples.

3. Folding the normalisation, and solving for the mask

Two passes replace the chain of section 1, and both write nothing. maskedStats() gathers the six masked moments (N, ΣI, ΣI², ΣT, ΣT², ΣIT) in one sweep; the means, both norms and the correlation of the centred vectors are algebra on those. The generated kernels then read the raw planes and do the masking (a masked pixel is skipped), the A−T recombination and the zero-mean centring per pixel. Eleven OpenCV calls and three planes per iteration go away; the arithmetic is unchanged, only the summation order, and the fixed point agrees with the previous layout to four digits in all four motion types.

The mask warp goes the same way when there is no user mask: preMask is then a rectangle, and its nearest-neighbour warp is, per row, an interval — for affine warps and for projective ones with a positive denominator alike. analyticMask() solves four linear inequalities per row instead of resampling a plane. Against the warped mask it differs on 0–2 pixels per warp over 500 random warps per motion type (the 1/1024 fixed-point ties of OpenCV's rounding); the homography basin is identical trial for trial.

4. Sigma as a parameter: the experiment, and what it left behind

The question was whether the pre-filter's sigma could be carried as an optimisation parameter next to the motion, starting from a large value so that the early iterations see a smoother objective. The heat equation gives its Jacobian column, ∂∂σ(Gσ ∗I)=σ∇2(Gσ ∗I), so the implementation is one more column. Five variants shared one upstream-layout implementation (src/fast_ecc_sigma.cpp on the sigma-param branch): sigma fixed, sigma estimated with the input-side column, the same with the template's derivative folded in, a geometric schedule, and the column solved for with sigma never moved.

  • Sigma does not shrink on its own. While the alignment is off, the correlation coefficient rewards blur — the autocorrelation broadens — and it is flat in sigma only at the solution. The symmetric formulation runs sigma to its cap; the input-side one moves it by +0.1 in the first step and then drifts slowly by a model artefact. "Start large and let it shrink" is not in the objective.
  • The column is what helps, and it helps with sigma fixed. The first step is identical whether or not sigma is updated (1 px → 0.070, against 0.183 without the column). σ∇²I has the shape of the isotropic second-order term of a misalignment residual, ½δT∇²Iδ, and of the bilinear interpolation error, h²∇²I/8 — two things the first-order model leaves in the residual and that a nuisance direction takes out of the motion estimate, the way the illumination scalar λ takes out a gain.
  • Sigma should stay near the default. With the column, sigma 0.8–1.1 is level with the plain kernels under 5 grey levels of noise (0.0197–0.0225 vs 0.0216 px); sigma 2 doubles the noise floor, column or not — blur costs gradient energy.

5. The laplacian column in the fused pass

Every kernel has a twin, GN<Name>L, with one more column: the 3×3 laplacian of the warped image, scaled by the pre-filter's sigma, whose coefficient is discarded. The generator now accumulates only the (u-pair, w-pair) sums the Gram matrix needs, which keeps the extra column at +5 to +7 live accumulators (affine 20 → 27). 6. As landed

The equivalence test now renders its input with a margin of real data around the template. The previous construction warped the template onto an image of its own size, which leaves a zero-filled strip along two edges that the pre-filter smears inward by gaussFiltSize/2; the sweep over gaussFiltSize was measuring how far each width's mask ring happened to sit from that strip (affine read 0.23 px at width 3, 0.002 px with a margin). With the margin every width sits on the floor, the sweep is flat, the tolerances come down to ~3× of it, and the whole sweep runs under all four flag sets.

6.2 A pyramid for fast-ecc

findTransformECC now takes a trailing nlevels (default 1). Above 1 it runs coarse to fine over a pyrDown pyramid the way eccms does: each level runs the whole single-scale iteration on the downsampled images and hands its warp to the next finer level scaled by S−1W S. A coarse level that gives up is skipped rather than fatal, and the finest level is the same iteration as before, so the floor is unchanged (analytic 0.0015 px, real image 0.0028 px, at 1, 2, 3 and 4 levels). 6.3 One parallel pass per iteration

After the folded normalisation an iteration was still five things in a row — OpenCV's warp, two gradient filters and a laplacian, the moment pass, the Gauss–Newton pass — each a fork/join, each writing a full-size plane for the next. It is now one parallel region over stripes of rows: each stripe samples its rows of the warped image straight from the blurred input (with a halo for the derivative stencils), takes the gradients and the laplacian in the stripe, solves for its mask rows, and accumulates the masked moments and the Gauss–Newton sums. Nothing full-size is written. The planes are centred on the previous iteration's means and the projections corrected exactly afterwards with the column sums of the Jacobian, Σ j, which the kernels now accumulate: for a column j and the true mean m, Σ j (i − m) = Σ j (i − mprev) − (m − mprev) Σ j. Stripes are four per thread — with one per thread a worker that wakes late stalls the region, and two threads were no faster than one.

6.5 The stripe, vectorised

The single-pass stage profile (eval/spbench.cpp: the library source included, the stripe body repeated with a timer between its stages) put the sampler at 25–35 % of a single-thread iteration, the derivative and moment loops at 20 % and the Gauss–Newton kernels at 45–60 %, all scalar. The three are now vectorised with OpenCV's universal intrinsics in their function form (OpenCV ≥ 4.7; older, or -DFASTECC_NO_SIMD, keeps the scalar loops, and CI runs the equivalence test on both).

The sampler. At 4.7 ns per pixel for affine — some 17 cycles — the scalar gather was well short of what the machine can do. Two things mattered. First, the shape of the loop: one pass per row that converts, indexes, gathers and combines each vector of pixels puts the conversion, the integer multiply and a store-to-load forward on the same dependency chain as the gathers, and the out-of-order window fills with that chain; two passes over chunks of 64 pixels — coordinates truncated and split into tap index and fractions into small buffers, then gathered and combined — leave nothing but the gathers and the weighting on the second pass's chain, and the bilinear combination as a weighted sum of the four taps (the weights depend only on the fractions and are ready before the taps arrive) shortens that chain from three dependent lerp steps to a multiply and two adds. Second, what an external build can reach: OpenCV's headers enable only SSE2 (or NEON) outside the library's own build, so there are no gather instructions — a tap vector is four scalar loads and three shuffles, about 5.5 loads per pixel in all, and the pass is bound by the two load ports at about 4 cycles per pixel where the taps stay in L1. A rotation spreads the taps of one row over some twenty source rows at 512 px and costs another 50 %. The projective row went from double to single precision like the affine one.

The kernels. Their inner loops are floating-point reductions, and under /fp:precise MSVC will not reassociate a sum, so it does not vectorise them (every one reports C5002; ivdep and __restrict change nothing, and spelling out four scalar partial sums is 1.6× slower because the arrays go to memory). The generator now emits, for each of the eight kernels, a vector path ahead of the scalar loop: one partial sum per lane for every live accumulator, the mask applied as a bitwise AND on the gradient inputs — every u, j and product downstream of a zeroed gradient is zero, so a masked lane contributes nothing, and the AND is safe on whatever the gradient planes hold outside the mask — and the lanes reduced into the scalar row accumulators before the tail. The reassociation is the one the kernels already made, float within a row and double at row end, one level down; four shorter chains lose less than one long one. The derivative, laplacian and moment loops of the stripe became one vector loop over the mask interval. And with the whole stripe in vectors the cache shows: four stripes per thread on one thread too, so that a stripe's planes stay in L2 between the sampler, the derivative loop and the kernel, takes the kernel pass of translation from 0.53 to 0.35 ms at 512.

6.6 Eight lanes, at run time

What stops the 128-bit kernel is not the algebra. The vector loop of the affine kernel with the laplacian column compiles (MSVC, SSE2) to 181 instructions per four pixels: 76 floating-point operations, 88 register moves — 56 of them spills and reloads of the 32 lane accumulators over 16 registers, the rest the copies that two-operand SSE encoding needs — and a handful of loads. That is issue-bound at about 45 cycles per four pixels, with the two floating-point ports (38 cycles) close behind; the measured 12 cycles per pixel is that. Splitting the loop into a Gram sweep and a projection sweep would recompute the gradients and change nothing else. Hoisting the x·y Gram column to the row end (it is y times the x column, exactly) took three accumulators and six operations off; what is left needs wider lanes and fused multiply-adds.

An external build does not get them from OpenCV: its headers enable SSE2 alone outside its own build, whatever the compiler was given, because the feature macros come from a generated file the dispatch machinery owns. The macros can be set by hand. -DFAST_ECC_AVX2=ON compiles with /arch:AVX2 (or -mavx2 -mfma) and defines CV_AVX2, CV_FMA3 and the SSE3–4.2 macros ahead of the headers, which then bring in the 256-bit universal intrinsics; the vector code, written width-agnostically (vx_load, vlanes()), needs no other change and runs at eight lanes with fused multiply-adds and hardware gathers. OpenCV 4.10's wrapper layer and 4.15's native functions both compile it; the equivalence test passes under it and the fixed points are unchanged to four digits. A binary built that way needs an AVX2 CPU, so the default build does what OpenCV's own dispatch does: it compiles the file twice — src/fast_ecc_avx2.cpp is the same source with the AVX2 flags — and findTransformECC picks the eight-lane instance once per process when cv::checkHardwareSupport reports AVX2 and FMA3. The 128-bit instance stays the fallback for older CPUs, FASTECC_NO_AVX2 in the environment forces it (which is how the two are compared in one binary), and vectorPath() reports which one runs. The dispatched instance is as fast as the whole library compiled for AVX2 (1.26 against 1.23 ms per iteration at 512, one thread).

7. What was tried, and what is left

  • A second-order model in the parameters (the image Hessian, inner iterations without a re-warp) was derived and not built. The blurred image's Taylor radius is about a pixel, and within it the first-order model with a good gradient already lands at 0.06–0.08 px in one warp; the second-order term would save at most one warp against three extra planes and a heavier kernel. The laplacian column is the cheap part of that model — its isotropic term, as one column.
  • A first-order-in-space approximation of the projective coordinate (a piecewise-affine sampler for homography) is not accurate enough for the residual: the chord error of a span S is aεS²/4, first order in the projective parameter, 3.5·10−3 px already at S = 8 against a floor of 2.6·10−3. A second-order (forward-differenced) coordinate would do, and the per-pixel division itself is only ~3 ns; the in-situ cost of warpPerspective and of the mask warp is the larger target, and the mask part is now gone.
  • The stop criterion |Δρ| < ε spends iterations settling ρ after the motion has reached its floor, which is why "3 iterations instead of 5" became 1.2× rather than 1.6× in call time. The paper stops on ‖Δp‖.
  • Homography's remaining rate (0.11 with the 5-tap, against 0.06 for affine) is the constant-A recombination; the fused kernel already has the per-pixel denominator, so a per-pixel Jacobian is a few operations away.

8. Summary

  1. The first-order model has exactly one estimated quantity, the gradient, and its bias is the convergence rate. A 5-tap takes the floor from 4–5 iterations to 3; nothing else in the model can be improved without leaving the model.
  2. Bookkeeping was half the iteration. Two passes that write nothing take 1.34–1.56× off it, exactly.
  3. Sigma cannot be estimated, but its column can be kept. As a nuisance direction it absorbs the isotropic second-order residual and the interpolation error: half the first step, +10–40 % basin, 2.6× lower error on a real image, for +2–19 % per iteration.
  4. One parallel region per iteration, sampling exactly. Single-thread cost is unchanged, four threads gain 1.6–2.1× over the multi-pass layout, and the 1/32 px rounding of OpenCV's warp — which was the translation floor and a systematic centre offset — is gone.
  5. The stripe, vectorised. The sampler (two passes per row, a weighted-sum bilinear: 1.9–2.4×), the derivative and moment loops (2.3×) and the eight Gauss–Newton kernels, whose vector path the generator now emits (2.5–3.3×), all with SSE2 alone: 2.4–2.8× on the single-thread iteration, and — the whole stripe rather than one stage competing with the thread pool — 1.9× on four threads too.
  6. Eight lanes, at run time. The 128-bit kernel is issue-bound (181 instructions per four pixels, a third of them spills); a second instance compiled with the AVX2 flags sets the feature macros OpenCV's headers would not, the same source runs at eight lanes with FMA, and the default build picks it at run time: 8.0–8.2× per iteration against ecc.cpp on one thread and 11.6–17.3× on four, 1.06–2.65× of eccms in the bench.
  7. Together, against a border-fixed ecc.cpp: 4.9–5.8× per iteration on one thread and 9.3–10.3× on four (affine, windows 256–768), 4.8–16× per call on one thread and 4.9–24× on four in the bundled bench, and a lower floor rather than an equal one. Against eccms.cpp fast-ecc is 2.0–2.6× faster on the real image whatever the start and 2.3× more accurate; in the bench, whose start is far off, 1.26–1.87× on one thread and 1.10–1.69× on four, single-scale against single-scale 2.0–3.3×.
Measured on an Intel Core i7-8700K (6C/12T), Windows 11, MSVC, Release, against OpenCV 4.15.0-dev built with the border fix applied. Single-thread timings on this machine vary by ±10–30 % between runs; every ratio is taken within one run, and per-iteration costs are the best of three interleaved runs.
